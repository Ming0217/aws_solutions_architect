Create Space

Before engaging your rational mind, check whether your biological state and psychological defaults are compromising your judgment. Most bad decisions are made before analysis even starts.

HALT Check

Stakes Assessment

1
Define the Problem

The most critical step. If you solve the wrong problem, nothing else matters. Write in plain language — if you need jargon, you don't understand the problem well enough.

Definition Principle
Take responsibility for defining the problem yourself. Don't accept the first framing someone else provides.
Root Cause Principle
Ask: "What would have to be true for this problem not to exist?" If your solution won't pass the test of time, you're treating a symptom.
Written Outputs
Max 2 sentences. Plain language. No jargon.
What changed or what deadline makes this decision necessary right now?
What is the underlying cause, not the visible symptom?
Defining boundaries prevents scope creep and hidden reframings by your defaults.
Facts / Assumptions / Unknowns

Separate what you know from what you're guessing. Many bad decisions come from treating speculation as fact, desire as forecast, or possibility as probability.

What do you know for certain? What has been confirmed by evidence?
What do you believe is true but haven't confirmed? Rate your confidence (low/medium/high).
What don't you know that could materially change your decision?
2
Explore Possible Solutions

Force yourself to generate at least 3 genuine options. Binary thinking (stay or leave, buy or don't) is almost always a sign you haven't explored the problem deeply enough. Include a "do nothing" option and at least one hybrid.

3+ Principle
If you only see two options, you're trapped. Force a third. Often the real answer is: delay, partial commitment, trial version, combine options, or redefine the objective.
Second-Level Thinking
For each option ask "And then what?" Your future self wants different choices than your today self. Trace the consequences forward 1, 3, and 5 years.
Bad Outcome Principle
Don't just imagine the ideal. Imagine what goes wrong and how you recover. Failure to imagine failure is itself a common cause of failure.

3
Evaluate the Options

Apply criteria to your options with rigor. Get high-quality information from sources close to the problem. Quantify where possible — vague criteria produce vague decisions.

Targeting Principle
Know what you're looking for before sorting through data. Most information is irrelevant. Identify the variables that probabilistically govern the outcome.
HiFi Principle
Get information close to the source, unfiltered by other people's biases. The person closest to the problem has the most accurate info — what they lack is broader perspective.
HiEx Principle
Seek people who recently solved a similar problem. Ask how they think, not what to do. Ask: "What variables would you use? What do most people miss?"
Criteria & Ranking
Write one per line. The top one is your single most important criterion. If you can't pick one, you haven't thought hard enough.
At what point does your top criterion flip the decision? Define the number or condition.
Base Rate / Reference Class

Before trusting your narrative, check what usually happens to people in this situation. Base rates ground your expectations in reality rather than optimism.

Name the reference class. e.g. "Mid-career professionals leaving stable jobs for startups"
Failure rate, median outcome, typical timeline to success, common failure modes.
Be honest: is this belief evidence-based or ego-based? What specific evidence supports your exceptionalism?
Information Quality
Who did you consult? What did they say? How recently did they solve a similar problem?
What single piece of missing information would most change your decision if you had it?
Confidence Calibration
Express as a percentage. If above 90%, ask: what am I not seeing? If below 60%, ask: what more do I need?
4
Decide and Execute

You've evaluated the options. Now commit. There is no purpose to knowing what you should do and not doing it. Make the judgment, write it down, and define the execution path.

Stop, FLOP, Know
Stop gathering when you've stopped learning new things. FLOP — act before your First Lost OPportunity. Know — act when a critical piece of information makes it obvious.
The Decision
One sentence. Unambiguous. Someone else should be able to read this and know exactly what you're doing.
Reference your top criterion and decision boundary from Stage 3.
Execution
What is the very next thing you will physically do? Make it specific and time-bound.
Pre-Commitment Check

After reflection, do you still endorse this decision across logic, emotion, and lived intuition — while recognizing that discomfort alone is not disproof?

Write your reasoning tonight. Re-read it tomorrow. Does it still hold up in the morning? Note what changed or what still feels off.
Execution Fail-Safes
Define in advance: "If [condition] by [date], I will [action]." No renegotiating in the moment.
Name the specific conditions under which you'd reverse this decision. If nothing would change your mind, you're not deciding — you're rationalizing.
If someone else needs to make decisions on your behalf during execution, what's the single most important thing they should optimize for?

5
Margin of Safety

When failure is expensive, invest in margins of safety. You need a margin of safety most at the very moment you start to think you don't. Prepare for the future — don't predict it.

Baseline Rule
Your margin of safety should absorb double the worst-case scenario. If your worst case takes 6 months to recover from, prepare for 12.
Bullets Before Cannonballs
Take small, low-risk steps toward multiple options before committing everything to one. Calibrate with cheap experiments, then fire the cannonball.
Quantified Risk Assessment
Be specific: dollars, months, reputation damage, opportunity cost. Not "things go badly" — quantify it.
Double every number above. Can you survive this? If not, your margin is insufficient.
Risk Mitigation
What small, low-risk step could you take to validate your assumptions before going all in?
What specifically will you do if the worst case materializes? Write the plan now while you're calm and clear-headed.
Is a pattern continuing just because it hasn't broken yet? What could disrupt the trend I'm relying on?
6
Learn from the Decision

Come back to this stage after the outcome is known. Judge the quality of your decision by your process, not the result. A good decision can have a bad outcome (bad luck), and a bad decision can have a good outcome (good luck). Only by reviewing your reasoning can you separate skill from chance.

Process Principle
Evaluate the process you used, not the outcome. Confidence increases faster than accuracy — more data makes us feel sure without making us more right.
Transparency Principle
Your ego distorts memory. Only written records are reliable. That's why you wrote everything down in the stages above.
Outcome Review
Copy from your earlier stages — what outcome were you anticipating and on what timeline?
Be factual. What was the actual outcome? What timeline did it follow?
Were my causal models correct? Did I predict the mechanism, or just get lucky/unlucky?
Process Grade
How much of the outcome was due to your process vs. factors beyond your control?
What will you do differently in your next major decision based on this experience?
